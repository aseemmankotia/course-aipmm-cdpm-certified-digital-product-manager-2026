## Chapter 3: Continuous Discovery and Opportunity Framing — Practice Questions

**Q1.** A product team has a clear outcome metric and is now building their opportunity-solution tree. They have generated three customer opportunities from interviews but jump straight to designing a solution for the opportunity that seems easiest to build. What is the most likely consequence of this approach?

A) The team risks solving a low-value opportunity because they selected based on build ease rather than opportunity impact and evidence strength
B) The team will ship faster because they skipped unnecessary opportunity evaluation
C) The team's opportunity-solution tree will have too many branches and become unusable
D) The team violates the desirability assumption by not testing viability first

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Opportunity selection in the tree should be driven by evidence of customer value and alignment to the outcome, not by which opportunity is easiest to build a solution for — this is a form of solution-first bias applied at the opportunity level.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Speed is not the risk being tested; the risk is picking the wrong opportunity, which wastes speed on the wrong target
- Tree size/usability is not the primary consequence described in the scenario
- Viability is one of three assumption types (desirability, viability, feasibility) but the scenario is about opportunity selection criteria, not assumption testing order

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** A discovery team wants to establish a continuous discovery cadence per Teresa Torres' framework. Which practice BEST reflects the recommended habit for weekly customer contact?

A) Conduct one large quarterly research study with 40 customers
B) Survey the entire customer base once a month and treat aggregate results as ongoing discovery
C) Rely on the sales team's anecdotal feedback instead of direct customer conversations
D) Interview at least one customer every week, on an ongoing basis, tied to the product trio's decisions

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Continuous discovery habits emphasize weekly touchpoints with customers by the core product trio (PM, designer, engineer), not periodic batch research.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Quarterly batch studies are traditional research, not continuous discovery
- Monthly aggregate surveys lack the qualitative, ongoing nature required for continuous discovery
- Secondhand sales feedback does not substitute for direct, ongoing customer conversations

</details>

**Q3.** A PM writes: 'Users need a dashboard with real-time analytics.' A discovery coach flags this as not being a testable problem statement. What is the core issue?

A) The statement is too long and should be shortened for clarity
B) The statement lacks a specific metric target
C) The statement should include a persona name to be valid
D) The statement describes a solution (dashboard) rather than an underlying problem or friction point that can be validated

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

A testable problem statement should describe the customer's underlying need or friction, not prescribe a solution — 'dashboard with real-time analytics' is a solution, which pre-commits to an approach before the problem is validated.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Length is not the issue being tested here
- Missing metrics is a secondary concern, not the primary flaw the coach identified
- Persona attribution is not required for a statement to be testable; the solution-first framing is the defect

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q4.** During journey mapping, a team identifies a step where 60% of users abandon the flow while searching for pricing information. According to opportunity-solution tree practice, what should this friction point become?

A) A solution to build immediately, such as adding a pricing page redesign
B) An opportunity node placed under the relevant outcome, to be explored before jumping to solutions
C) A new outcome metric replacing the current one
D) An experiment to run without further opportunity framing

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Journey map friction points are sources of opportunities; they should be added as opportunity nodes under the outcome in the tree and explored (e.g., via interviews) before solutions are designed.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Jumping straight to a solution skips opportunity validation, a classic solution-first bias trap
- Friction points inform opportunities, not outcomes — outcomes are the overarching metric goals
- Running an experiment without first framing the opportunity skips the tree's structure and risks testing the wrong thing

</details>

**Q5.** A team is assumption mapping a new solution idea. Which of the following BEST represents a viability assumption (as opposed to desirability or feasibility)?

A) The revenue generated from this feature will exceed the cost to build and maintain it
B) Customers will click the new button when they see it
C) The engineering team can build the integration within the current sprint capacity
D) The feature is technically compatible with the existing API architecture

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Viability assumptions test whether the solution makes business sense — that revenue/value exceeds cost — distinct from desirability (customer want) and feasibility (can we build it).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- This is a desirability assumption — will customers want/use it
- This is a feasibility assumption — can the team build it
- This is also a feasibility assumption — technical compatibility

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q6.** A product trio has limited discovery bandwidth and must choose one opportunity from five identified in the opportunity-solution tree to pursue this quarter. Which selection approach is MOST aligned with continuous discovery best practice?

A) Pick the opportunity with the strongest evidence of customer impact and clearest alignment to the outcome metric
B) Pick the opportunity the loudest stakeholder requested
C) Pick all five opportunities simultaneously to maximize coverage
D) Pick the opportunity that requires the least engineering effort, regardless of customer evidence

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

With limited bandwidth, opportunities should be prioritized based on evidence strength and alignment to the desired outcome, not stakeholder volume, effort minimization, or attempting to cover everything at once.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Stakeholder pressure is not a discovery-evidence-based prioritization criterion
- Pursuing all five simultaneously contradicts the premise of limited bandwidth and dilutes focus
- Effort alone ignores customer value evidence, risking a low-impact but easy choice

</details>

**Q7.** A team has designed a solution before conducting any customer interviews, then seeks interviews only to confirm the solution is good. What discovery anti-pattern does this represent?

A) Continuous discovery habit
B) Assumption mapping
C) Opportunity framing
D) Solution-first bias

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Solution-first bias occurs when a team commits to a solution before validating the underlying problem/opportunity, then seeks confirming (not disconfirming) evidence.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Continuous discovery habit refers to the healthy ongoing cadence of customer contact, the opposite of this anti-pattern
- Assumption mapping is the practice of identifying desirability/viability/feasibility risks, not the anti-pattern itself
- Opportunity framing is the correct upstream step being skipped in this scenario

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q8.** In the opportunity-solution tree, what is the correct hierarchical order from top to bottom?

A) Outcome → Opportunities → Solutions → Experiments
B) Solutions → Opportunities → Outcome → Experiments
C) Experiments → Solutions → Opportunities → Outcome
D) Opportunities → Outcome → Experiments → Solutions

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

The tree starts with a single desired outcome at the root, branches into opportunities (customer needs/friction), then solutions addressing those opportunities, then experiments to test the solutions.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- This reverses the hierarchy, starting with solutions before opportunities and outcome are defined
- This is fully inverted from the correct top-down structure
- This misorders outcome and experiments relative to opportunities and solutions

</details>
