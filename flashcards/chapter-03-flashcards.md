## Chapter 3: Continuous Discovery and Opportunity Framing — Flashcards

| # | Front (Question) | Back (Answer) |
|---|-----------------|---------------|
| 1 | Opportunity-solution tree: what are the 4 levels, top to bottom? | Outcome → Opportunities → Solutions → Experiments. Exam tip: outcome is singular (one root metric); everything below must trace back to it. |
| 2 | Continuous discovery habit: recommended customer contact frequency (Teresa Torres framework)? | At least weekly — one customer touchpoint per week, ongoing, involving the product trio (PM, designer, engineer). |
| 3 | What are the three assumption types in assumption mapping? | Desirability (will customers want it), Feasibility (can we build it), Viability (does it make business sense/cost vs. value). |
| 4 | Solution-first bias — definition and exam trap | Designing/committing to a solution before validating the underlying problem/opportunity. Exam trap: scenario shows a team jumping straight to building or seeking only confirming feedback. |
| 5 | Validated problem vs. assumed problem — key distinguishing test | A validated problem has direct evidence from customer interviews/behavior (e.g., observed friction, stated pain repeated across interviews). An assumed problem is based on internal opinion, stakeholder guesses, or unverified hypotheses. |
| 6 | Journey map friction point → tree placement | Friction points identified in a journey map become Opportunity nodes in the opportunity-solution tree, not solutions. They must be explored before solution design. |
| 7 | Testable problem statement — what to avoid | Avoid naming a solution in the statement (e.g., 'users need a dashboard'). Instead describe the friction/need (e.g., 'users can't quickly determine pricing before deciding to upgrade'). |
| 8 | Opportunity selection under limited discovery bandwidth — best criterion | Prioritize by strength of customer evidence and alignment to the outcome metric — not by stakeholder volume, ease of build, or attempting to cover all opportunities at once. |
| 9 | Who makes up the 'product trio' in continuous discovery? | Product Manager, Designer, and Engineer — the trio jointly conducts weekly customer touchpoints and shares discovery findings. |
| 10 | Desirability assumption — example | "Customers will want/use this feature." Tests customer demand, separate from feasibility (build) and viability (business case). |
| 11 | Viability assumption — example | "Revenue/value generated exceeds cost to build and maintain." Tests the business case, not customer want or technical build capability. |
| 12 | Common exam trap: interviews conducted quarterly in bulk — is this continuous discovery? | No. Continuous discovery requires an ongoing, weekly cadence, not periodic large-batch research studies. Batch quarterly research is traditional research, not continuous discovery. |
